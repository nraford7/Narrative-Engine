# Educational briefing: direct explanation

A fictional walkthrough, not a recorded evaluation. This example uses the current standalone process.

## Source

An internal survey of 60 volunteers reported that 38 preferred remote work for concentrated tasks and 34 preferred being together for onboarding. Responses could include both. The sample was voluntary; productivity, retention and the effects of policy changes were not measured.

## Assignment first

**Mode:** Deep, explicitly chosen. **Purpose:** educate. **Success:** managers can distinguish task preferences from evidence about productivity. **Audience:** mixed managers who currently treat one office policy as best for every task. That belief is supplied by the user, not inferred from the category. **Format/use:** standalone prose briefing, 600–800 words.

## Material Read

The two preferences can coexist because people could select both. This matters to an audience assuming one universal preference. The voluntary sample describes respondents; it cannot establish company-wide preference or productivity. There is no demonstrated policy effect. The strongest passages are the two task-specific responses and the explicit measurement limits. No surprise is assumed without knowing what these readers have seen.

## Treatment and provisional points

Standard detail, informed readers, conversational rhythm, balanced tone. Candidate A: “Task preferences differ; they do not establish which policy improves productivity.” Candidate B: “The survey is a starting point for questions, not a policy verdict.” Both carry the sample limitations.

## Structure comparison

**Direct explanation:** state the distinction; explain overlapping responses; separate preference from outcomes; close with what the survey leaves unanswered. This fits the educational purpose and short length.

**Rashomon considered and rejected:** two response counts do not supply full competing accounts, cross-examination and an evidenced synthesis. Do not invent interviews or organizational conflict to fill essential beats.

## Joint approval and brief

The user selects A with direct explanation. The brief retains the audience belief, educational success condition, voluntary sample, nonexclusive answers, length and no unsupported productivity claim. The opening can state the distinction directly.

The writer must explain why a preference is not an outcome measure. A final paragraph demanding an office-policy change would fail this assignment, even if its tone were polished.

## Complete approved brief

**Purpose + success condition:** Inform / Educate. Managers should explain why task preferences can coexist and why neither establishes a productivity effect. Ask: understand these distinctions, not approve a workplace policy.

**Audience + starting position:** Mixed managers. User-supplied belief: one office policy is best for every task. Their knowledge of survey design is unknown; define voluntary sampling briefly. Do not assume hostility to remote work.

**Format + intended use + limits:** Standalone prose briefing; target 600–800 words without padding. Include both counts and measurement limits. No external research or invented participant quotations.

**Focal Statement:**
- One Thing: Task preferences differ; they do not establish which policy improves productivity.
- Ask: distinguish preferences, overlapping task needs and measured outcomes.
- Through-Line: read the two responses together, explain the sample, then identify what conclusions remain unavailable.
- `focal_origin: user-selected` — the user chose candidate A, not merely approved an inferred brief.

**Material Read:** The source says “38 preferred remote work for concentrated tasks” and “34 preferred being together for onboarding”; “Responses could include both.” These passages address the assumption that one setting must fit all tasks. “The sample was voluntary; productivity, retention and the effects of policy changes were not measured” limits any recommendation. Stakes: sound interpretation. Tension: task preferences versus a single universal policy. Surprise: none established for this audience. What changes: managers distinguish task-specific preference from outcome evidence. Missing: representative sampling, measured effects and costs. The two counts are not opposing camps or contradictory votes.

**Argument Outline:**
1. State the distinction between preference and outcomes; support with the two preference findings and unmeasured outcomes.
2. Explain that choices could overlap; quote “Responses could include both.” This resolves apparent conflict without inventing respondent combinations.
3. Explain voluntary sampling; it describes respondents and cannot establish everyone's preferences. Transition from what was answered to whom the answers represent.
4. Explain the missing outcome measures; no productivity or retention conclusion follows. Close with what readers can and cannot infer, not a policy directive.

**Shape + Register:** answer-first / prose. Direct explanation best fits the modest educational task. Rashomon rejected because full competing accounts and an evidenced synthesis are absent. No named arc or omitted essential beat.

**Writing treatment:** Standard detail, informed audience with sampling knowledge unknown, conversational rhythm, balanced tone. Use concrete distinctions and clear sentences. Do not impose a named voice's rhetorical quota.

**Opening opportunity:** “A preference for remote focus and a preference for in-person onboarding can coexist.” Follow immediately with the source's nonexclusive response rule; do not claim the same specific respondents chose both.

**Evidence boundaries:** No causal productivity claims, company-wide extrapolation, invented quotations or policy-effect estimates. Preserve sample size, voluntary selection and unmeasured outcomes.

**Success checks:** Reader can explain nonexclusive responses, distinguish preference from outcomes, and state the survey's limits. Remain within the target length without repetition.

**Pre-build consistency check:** Purpose and Ask agree; sections address the known belief; each factual inference has support; unsupported policy conclusions are excluded; prose use and treatment fit; the user has approved this point and structure together.
